# Stakeholder and design-feedback findings

## Purpose and evidence sources

This document summarizes two anonymized feedback sources shared with the
project team. Their participant overlap is unknown, so their counts are
reported separately and must not be added together.

1. An earlier team feedback summary reported nine respondents, most of whom
   were researchers. It did not provide enough detail to verify recruitment,
   session format, or questions asked.
2. A later online design-feedback survey summary reported 13 respondents.
   The supplied summary does not establish recruitment method, collection
   dates, or whether any respondents also took part in the earlier feedback.

These are descriptive stakeholder/design feedback, not usability-test
outcomes, SUS scores, or a fairness evaluation. Do not treat them as
representative estimates.

## Earlier team feedback summary (n=9)

The earlier summary reported:

- 7 of 9 respondents described consent forms as long or difficult to use.
- 5 of 9 raised guardian/child consent issues and related ethics delays.
- 3 of 9 raised language barriers, 3 of 9 raised literacy concerns, and 3 of
  9 raised field connectivity or power constraints.

Themes also included plain-language explanations of consent and participant
rights; discoverability of language and read-aloud support; simpler visual
presentation; clear approval and translation sign-off states; offline
recovery; and audio records linked to the correct consent record. Paper-form
import and witnessed-consent guidance were questions to explore.

The earlier respondent group did not include ethics-office staff or
interpreters, according to the available summary.

## Online design-feedback survey (n=13)

All 13 supplied responses indicated agreement to take part in the feedback
survey. Role selections were 8 student researchers, 3 staff researchers or
supervisors, 1 research participant/community member, and 1 past student.
No ethics committee/office staff or interpreters/community liaisons were
represented.

The question about consent-form experiences allowed multiple selections.
Counts below use 13 respondents as the denominator and therefore do not sum
to 13:

| Reported experience | Responses |
|---|---:|
| Forms too long or difficult | 8/13 |
| Slow ethics approval because of form mistakes | 6/13 |
| Consent involving children/guardians | 5/13 |
| Literacy difficulties | 4/13 |
| Formatting for an institution's template | 4/13 |
| Form unavailable in participant's language | 3/13 |
| No internet or electricity in the field | 3/13 |
| Paper forms lost, damaged, or hard to store | 1/13 |
| Oral consent records difficult to keep with other recordings | 1/13 |

Ten respondents answered the open-ended question about a difficult
experience. The supplied responses raised themes including language
mismatch, long forms and approval delays, lost forms, literacy or signing
barriers, limited progress visibility, and mistrust or misunderstanding.
These are themes from a small set of self-reports, not verified incident
rates.

Suggested tool capabilities included plain-language forms, local-language
read-aloud, recording participant answers, approval progress tracking,
physical-form upload, electronic signatures, and clearer review and approval
steps. A proposed three-day approval target is an individual suggestion, not
an agreed service level.

Ten respondents provided comments on the images. The supplied comments
included requests for clearer language and read-aloud support, better
legibility and visual cohesion, and more explanation of some design
elements. Comments also noted that colors could affect how realistic or
preferable a concept appeared. Image 6 received both positive comments and
criticism about text size, complexity, and visual busyness; the rating counts
alone do not resolve those concerns.

In response to whether the designs reflected each respondent's idea, 3 of 13
answered “all of it,” 1 “most of it,” 5 “some of it,” 2 “none of it,” and 2
said they had not described a tool. Eight people answered what was missing
or should change first. Their suggestions included approval-progress
tracking, local languages, voice support, clearer descriptions, layout
changes, and a less cartoon-like visual style.

## Design implications

Use these findings as hypotheses for design and follow-up research:

1. Prioritize concise, plain-language consent content, and validate
   comprehension rather than assuming readability from visual ratings.
2. Make language choice and read-aloud support visible. Validate this with
   people who use the relevant languages and accessibility features.
3. Improve visual hierarchy and legibility, especially text size and
   consistency; test color treatments without implying that a concept is
   already functional.
4. Explore status visibility for ethics review and researcher feedback, but
   validate the process with ethics-office staff before promising a
   turnaround time.
5. Explore safe links between audio consent records and the corresponding
   record, plus offline recovery and physical-form workflows, with privacy
   and ethics review.
6. Include guardian/child flows and signing alternatives in future research;
   do not treat stakeholder suggestions as approved policy.

These implications do not demonstrate that a design change will solve a
reported problem.

## Limitations and next steps

The 13-person survey was dominated by student researchers and did not include
ethics-office staff or interpreters/community liaisons. Its recruitment
method, collection dates, and relationship to the earlier nine-person
feedback summary are unknown. Multiple-choice experience counts may overlap.
The image-rating totals provided cover 12 ratings per image, not 13; the
missing response should be checked against the approved source before formal
publication. Open-ended response themes are summarized without respondent
quotes or counts because the supplied export did not provide a reliable
theme-coding method.

Recruit ethics/translation stakeholders and participants with varied
language, literacy, and device experience for follow-up. Keep usability
task outcomes and any SUS data in their own analysis, with methods,
denominators, build limitations, and privacy review. Neither this survey nor
the earlier feedback establishes usability, comprehension, accessibility,
fairness, causality, or statistical significance.

## Privacy and use

This summary intentionally excludes respondent-level records, direct
identifiers, verbatim free-text answers, and re-identification details. Keep
raw responses, recordings, contact details, and any linkage key in the
team's approved restricted storage—not in Git, issues, or public reports.
